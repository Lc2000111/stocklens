Cheap or expensive vs. their own history

The S&P 500, Nasdaq-100 and Dow 30, ranked by where today's valuation sits within its own range over the past 5 years. A 90th-percentile stock is more expensive than it was 90% of the time over that period; a 10th-percentile stock is cheaper than it was 90% of the time.

How it's calculated

Trailing P/E (daily, Yahoo) = closing price ÷ trailing-12-month EPS, where trailing EPS is the sum of the four most recently reported quarters as of that day. Rebuilt for every trading day over 5 years.

Forward P/E (monthly, I/B/E/S via WRDS) = price ÷ analysts' consensus EPS for the next twelve months. That's a blend of this fiscal year's and next fiscal year's consensus, weighted by how much of this year is left, which is the standard way forward P/E is quoted. Each month uses the estimates analysts actually had at that time, so the history isn't distorted by hindsight.

PEG (monthly, I/B/E/S) = forward P/E ÷ expected EPS growth, where growth = next fiscal year's consensus ÷ this year's − 1. Shown as n/m when expected growth is below 2% (dividing by a near-zero growth rate gives a huge, meaningless PEG). I/B/E/S also has a long-term growth estimate, but only 1–4 analysts provide it per stock, so it's shown only in the tooltip.

Percentile = share of the past 5 years (days for trailing, months for forward and PEG) with a value at or below today's. Today's forward P/E and PEG use today's price with the latest I/B/E/S consensus. I/B/E/S arrives monthly with a lag; the date is shown above the table.

Expensive / Cheap. "Most extreme 20%" means a stock counts as expensive at the 80th percentile or above, and cheap at the 20th or below, against its own 5-year history.

Badges count how many of the three measures (trailing P/E, forward P/E and PEG) agree. ALL 3 HIGH means the stock looks expensive against its own history whichever way you measure it, and ALL 3 LOW means it looks cheap on all three. Outlined 2 OF 3 badges mean two agree; hover a badge to see which. One measure on its own can mislead: a high trailing P/E may just mean earnings are about to jump, and a low one may come from a one-time gain inflating EPS. So the more measures agree, the stronger the signal. PEG shows n/m (and can't count) when expected growth is under 2%.

Caveats. Trailing EPS uses the adjusted figures analysts track, so it can differ from the GAAP P/E on the Stock page. A high percentile can mean the price ran up or earnings/estimates fell; a low one can mean the price fell or earnings rose. Cheap isn't automatically a bargain: it can reflect real problems. Stocks with a spin-off in the window (⚠) are hidden by default because their earnings before and after aren't comparable. Forward and PEG percentiles need at least 24 months of I/B/E/S history; newer listings show "short". Refresh the I/B/E/S data monthly with .venv/bin/python ibes.py.

Data storage. Everything is saved in a SQLite database, stocklens.db. Each "Refresh prices" adds a snapshot, so you can see how valuations change over time. Edit the stock lists in universe.py; update the S&P 500 list with .venv/bin/python update_index_lists.py.
